# Editorial passes for college PS

Use only the passes the request needs. These are editorial heuristics synthesized
from the component skills and admissions sources, not university scoring criteria.

## 1. Substance and structure

Write a short reverse outline of what the existing paragraphs do. A paragraph
can establish context, show an experience, develop a thought, bridge time, or
reconsider an earlier belief. The question is what it contributes to knowing the
applicant; a plot summary alone misses that purpose.

Locate where the draft's claimed insight is actually supported. When it says
that the student gained confidence, locate the experience, decision, changed
interpretation, or behavior that makes this credible. If absent, ask for it.
An introspective essay may show the student's thinking without a dramatic action.

Assess proportion by its effect: has background crowded out the person applying
now? Has the draft spent its words naming the lesson but not examining it? Does
an activity contribute an insight, or duplicate the activities list? Cut or
compress according to that diagnosis, not a fixed past/present ratio.

For narratives involving other people, distinguish their story from the student's
relationship to it. Retain context necessary to understand the student's response.
For hardship, development need not mean that the original harm has been resolved.

## 2. Opening, sequence, and flow

An opening earns attention through a recognizable voice, a meaningful question,
a concrete situation, or an interesting thought. Suspense and in-medias-res are
options, not requirements. Give enough orientation that the reader does not have
to solve the facts before understanding the person.

Check the chronology independently of the telling order. When a draft opens
later and moves backward, make the time shift legible. The narrator can reflect
with present knowledge but should not silently give their younger self that same
understanding. Flag contradictions rather than choosing the more dramatic version.

Read each paragraph ending beside the next opening. The connection should be
understandable through an event, question, contrast, association, or changed
understanding. A new transition word cannot repair a missing causal link.
Preserve ambiguity in emotions while clarifying what physically happened.

Choose which moments need scene detail and which can be summarized. Develop
the moment carrying the insight; bridge routine history economically. Do not
inflate every paragraph into a cinematic scene or force a fiction plot structure.

## 3. Wording and imagery

Prefer a clear actor and accurate verb where the student knows the actor.
Keep passive phrasing when the actor is unknown or the focus warrants it.
Do not convert an uncertain outcome into a definite event to strengthen a verb.

Check words for literal meaning, connotation, idiom, and fit with the student's
register. An ornate synonym may introduce a claim the original did not support.
The best correction is often smaller than a replacement paragraph.

Preserve distinctive, true sensory details. Replace decorative abstraction with
facts already present, or ask for the missing observation. Never manufacture
texture. A figurative phrase earns space if it makes the experience or thought
more understandable. Multiple competing images can obscure it.

Watch for generic formulations such as claims about the power of perseverance
or the diversity of a new community. Ask what the student actually noticed,
thought, or did. Do not solve generic prose by inserting new impressive vocabulary.

## 4. Voice and rhythm

Before editing, note a few characteristics of the student's own writing: register,
usual vocabulary, sentence patterns, humor, directness, and how feelings appear.
An existing student paragraph is enough to begin; do not demand a writing corpus.
Do not identify AI authorship from punctuation or vocabulary.

For ESL light edits, distinguish three cases:

- **Keep:** an understandable unusual collocation, direct emotional statement,
  simple repetition, or uneven rhythm that belongs to the student's register.
  Greater fluency alone is not a reason to replace it.
- **Repair locally:** a tense, agreement, reference, or word-choice error that
  makes the reader stumble or infer the wrong event. Preserve the surrounding
  vocabulary and sentence shape where possible.
- **Ask about meaning:** an image or reflection with more than one plausible
  reading. Keep its expressive core while the student clarifies it; do not
  substitute a polished lesson or discard it simply because it sounds unusual.

Judge the effect in context, rather than setting an error quota or treating ESL
writers as a single voice. Explain material edits by the misunderstanding they
prevent. If a local edit needs a new motivation, insight, or image, it has become
a substantive rewrite; return to the student's intended meaning first. Leave
clear neighboring sentences alone, including newly supplied student wording.

Read aloud or simulate speaking to a person. Find where the thought becomes
hard to follow or the emphasis lands on an unimportant word. Vary length in
service of meaning, not by alternating short and long sentences mechanically.
Preserve deliberate repetition; remove accidental duplicated sentences.

Re-read the original opening and ending beside the edited versions. Has the
student become more formal, self-congratulatory, tidy, or certain than their
account supports? Restore the meaning and register. Correct errors without
staging slang, inserting mistakes, or stereotyping how a teenager should sound.

## 5. Compression and final checks

Work from largest to smallest: duplicate episode or digression, repeated
reflection, decorative explanation, redundant phrase, then local wordiness.
Keep the small details that do narrative work. An ending can close on a present
practice, an honest question, or an earned image; it need not promise to change
the world or return to the opening object.

Count the submission passage alone, excluding headings, comments, archives,
and duplicate working copies. A whitespace count is an estimate, particularly
around hyphens and dashes. The platform's accepted counter is the final check.

Useful edit note, when the recipient's rules permit the proposed operation:

| Location | Original or diagnosis | Proposed operation | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paragraph 2 | Two different time references | Ask student which event happened first | Fixes the sequence without inventing it |
| Paragraph 4 | Same accomplishment stated twice | Delete one repetition | Recovers words without losing content |
| Final paragraph | General claim about confidence | Ask for one changed choice or understanding | Gives the claim support |

Grammar alternatives can be shown directly when permitted. For structural
feedback under a rule that prohibits AI outlines, describe the existing reading
problem and ask the student to decide the revision, rather than composing a new
sequence of application paragraphs.
